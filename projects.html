supply_route

Risk, before the truck leaves.

A causal simulator and a dispatch-time model for supply corridors in Bangladesh. The districts are synthetic. The methodology is not. The project also asks how far a prediction can be trusted when the question turns causal.

One world, simulated. Then asked hard questions.

Every row comes from an explicit structural causal model, so the true effect is known. The predictor sees only what is available at dispatch. The causal module then checks its answers against that truth.

  1. 01

    Simulate

    Thirty-one districts over three thousand days. Weather, unrest, fuel and logistics follow an explicit graph, with confounding and persistence built in. One seed produces 93,000 rows.

  2. 02

    Predict

    A gradient-boosted model sees 42 columns known at dispatch. The newest 20% of days are held out behind a 30-day buffer, so autocorrelated weather cannot leak into the score.

  3. 03

    Interrogate

    Backdoor adjustment is compared with the simulator’s oracle. Naive comparisons miss by as much as a factor of three. Flipping an input inside the predictor erases the effect entirely.

The fine print.

The methods are real. The world is not. Coefficients encode assumptions, not measurements.

Targets
condition_class (normal, degraded, critical), condition_score (0 to 100), delay_hours
Validation
Newest 20% of days held out, with a 30-day buffer; expanding-window cross-validation for the rest
Metrics
Macro-F1, log loss and per-class recall; MAE, RMSE and R² for regression
Model
Gradient boosting, with rounds chosen on the newest 15% of each training window
Stack
Python, numpy, pandas, scipy, scikit-learn 1.4 or newer
Scope
31 synthetic districts, one product per route, same-day effects only
Known limits. No spatial spillover, no route network, no lagged effects. Class balance sits near 75 / 21 / 4, so the critical class is rare. None of this describes the real Bangladesh. It asks only whether the methods hold up in a world where the truth is visible.
